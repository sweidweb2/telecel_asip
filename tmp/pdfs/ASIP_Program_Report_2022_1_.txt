22% of Africa’s working age population 
are starting new businesses, which 
results in the highest startup rate 
worldwide. Coupled with projections 
that two-thirds of the world population 
growth in the next 30 years is set to be 
in Africa, it’s clear that the continent is 
primed to be the next global startup 
hub. 
Despite the clear opportunity, Africa’s 
growth rates and entrepreneurial 
m i n d s e t , A f r i c a n f o u n d e r s a r e 
constrained by signi ﬁcant structural 
challenges unique to each region. As 
they seek to innovate and provide 
solutions to intractable problems such 
as food security, ﬁnancial inclusion 
and energy dependency, they will 
require support to validate and scale 
their solutions. 
Startupbootcamp AfriTech scouts, 
skills, and scales disruptive pan-
African startups, ﬁnally allowing them  
to compete in global markets. We help 
founders validate their solutions by 
fostering collaboration with Africa’s 
most forward-thinking corporations. 
SBC AfriTech compresses 18 - 24 
months of growth into a 3-month 
programme and helps generate vast 
commercial traction. 
This cohort of SBC AfriTech has seen 
us evolve with our institutional 
collaborations. To foster our ﬁrst in-
person cohort in Dakar, we partnered 
with the Telecel Group and the 
Délégation Générale à l'Entreprenariat 
Rapide des Femmes et des Jeunes 
(DER/FJ). We employed a hybrid model, 
with the startups spending the ﬁrst and 
last three weeks of the program in-
person, and the intermediate six weeks 
remotely.  
SBC AfriTech is a proudly African 
solution with global brand-backing, 
with the ﬁrst ever instance where 
multiple corporations on the continent 
have embraced the concept of open 
innovation and collaboration to solve

Competitive Advantage
Corporate-Startup Collaboration
The competitive advantage behind the 
SBC model is Corp orate-Startup 
Collaboration (CSC). Our main focus is 
to   thrive  from  meaningful  
partnerships and synergies between 
emerging and established businesses.  
As the ﬁ rst multi-corporate-backed 
accelerator to launch in Africa in 2017, 
our partners have been able to ﬁnd new 
lines of revenue, provide solutions for 
their customers, gain innovation 
insight, improve internal processes, 
and leapfrog competition by innovating 
faster. 
The Covid-19 pandemic came with 
insurmountable challenges, chief 
among them being the disruption of all 
industry verticals. Covid accelerated 
the disruption across the entire value 
chain and has impacted every aspect 
including logistics, retail, agriculture, 
ﬁnance, credit, health, and education, to 
name a few. 
In the face of this paradigm shift, 
corporates struggle to adapt quickly. 
Large corporates have long decision-

stakeholders to consider. This creates 
fertile ground for startups to step in 
with radical new solutions and quick

African Startup Outlook
One can conclude that African Startups 
have a very promising future, but there 
are still key issues which need to be 
addressed. Still, only 21.5% of startups 
had female representation in the 
founding teams. Countries with the 
highest share of female representation 
were Kenya with 32.2%, Nigeria with 
24.2%, Ghana with 22.2%, South Africa 
with 14.6%, closely followed by Egypt, 
Morocco, and Tunisia with 13.9%, 13.3% 
and 13.3% respectively. Female 
empowerment is gaining popularity but 
at a slow pace (Disrupt Africa, 2022). 
Just 37.8% of startups participated in 
some kind of incubation or acceleration 
program of which many have female 
representation as requirement. Pushing 
these requirements and fostering 
f e m a l e f o u n d e d c o m p a n i e s i n 
particular, could result in visible 
changes in following years. The overall 
social impact still remains positive. 
From the reported startups, 17,915 Jobs 
were created in total, which are 32 new 
jobs per startup. The most impactful 
cluster developments were reported in 
Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya, and South Africa, 
of which 88.7% were African founded 
startups (Disrupt Africa, 2022). 
Another trend we will see in the future 
is that with increased funding and 
corporate-VC engagement, capital 
deepening will allow for more readily 
available resources. This development 
has been criticised with the question, if 
such access to capital would actually 
be bene ﬁcial for startups? Although 
capital deepening will always be a 
factor that fosters economic growth 
and will even effect the startups on an 
o p e r a t i o n a l l e v e l , i t i s n o t a 
determinant of economic development.  
Startups will be expected to show 
higher growth because of more access 
to capital. This however, could be a 
crucial mistake which may foster 
unproductive or even destructive 
entrepreneurship. Although ﬁnancial 
inclusion has been one of the most 
d e t r i m e nt a l i s s u e s f o r A f r i c a n 
entrepreneurs, other institutional 
factors still pose barriers. These de jure 
factors will not change as quick as 
corporates and VC might wish, and 
could cause perverse incentives for 
unsustainable decision making 
focused on growth. Thus, it is, and 
always be important to have the right 
medium for funding which could be  an 
accelerator or incubator.  
Fine dosage of knowledge and capital, 
p a i r e d w i t h i n d u s t r y s p e c i ﬁ c 
mentoring and consulting still remains 
the most effective and sustainable 
approach to foster entrepreneurial  and 
economic development. Prone to repeat 
history and debt intolerance, it is not 
the level of capital stock but the quality  
which determines the stability of many 
African countries. With this in mind, 
SBC AfriTech is aimed at fostering 
entrepreneurship, not just to achieve

continuous development. The latter is 
the dif ﬁcult part, but it also the most
